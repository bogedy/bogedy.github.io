## Why no births?
I recently saw a good article on Twitter that dispels some of the more handwavy explanations of the fertility crisis. It is not simply an issue of housing, education, or social services. Israel suffers from problems with all of these but has a high birthrate, even among secular people. They just like having kids more. Article here: https://x.com/MoreBirths/status/1870911221630685465

I want to add one bit: what about China? They have a very family-oriented culture, yet their plummeting birthrate suggests that they aren't all that excited to make families. I recall in the book *Wild Swans*, a memoir of three generations in China, that the author's grandmother lived in a home with five generations under one roof. This was so admirable to early 20th century Chinese people that their whole street was renamed to "Five Generations In One House Street."

What could explain how such family venerating people could come to make so few of them? Here I dispel some ideas:

Could it be that the Chinese communists waged a successful war against family institutions? For most of Chinese civilization, Chinese people were clan-oriented. This changed when the communists endeavored to replace every source of tradition and authority with the party. During the Cultural Revolution, streets like "Five Generations In One House Street" were given new names like "Class Struggle Street." Also, an underappreciated aspect of the one-child policy was that it de facto destroyed Han Chinese clans. 

This cannot be the explanation, because other East Asian countries without histories of communist revolutions also face plummeting birth rates. Korea shares the same family-oriented, Confucian culture as China. Taiwan is even more culturally similar to China, and also suffers from low birth rates.

Could the fertility crisis be explained by rapid economic growth? China has not only developed a great deal, they've developed in a very small amount of time. I recall sitting in traffic in the city of Hohhot, China in 2016. Everyone I met told me that just a few years back there were no cars on the road. The degree of change in a very short period of time was extreme. This could mean that family culture in China (and East Asia broadly) suffered a pronounced shock that it has yet to recover from. Such rapid economic growth can draw people away from their families. The 2009 documentary [*Last Train Home*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q07bMuQLrbY) depicts a daughter who leaves her family to work in a factory and find independence, taking after her parents who also left to work in a factory to provide for her. Lax regulations in coastal Special Economic Zones encourage mass migration *of parents*, while the Hukou household registration system ties citizens' public service rights to their hometowns. So children often remain in the countryside to be raised by their grandparents. The mass migration of people from the countryside to the cities rips families and local ties apart. 

The point here is simply that change of any kind can be destructive. It is intuitive to me that great, rapid changes in China could have taken the birthrate as a casualty, for complicated reasons.

I do not know exactly how Israel compares in this regard. Israel has had high GDP growth rates, but nothing like the extreme experience of China. Today Israelis live very modern, comfortable lives, but at the onset of its existence, a year before the establishment of the PRC, Israel was a very poor country of immigrants displaced by war and persecution. It was not until decades and a few wars later that it began to integrate into the global economy. So I think we can say that there is a certain rapidness to Israel's development, even if it doesn't compare to China's. Still, Israelis love to have kids.

I think that the difference is cultural.

## Why so serious?
As someone who has spent a lot of time in China and in Israel, I suppose the birthrate gap makes sense: Jews are humorous and passionate about having kids, while Chinese people can be humorless and strict about it. Let me explain:

In dynastic China, filial piety was the law. You could be put to death for sufficiently disrespecting your parents. Much of the Confucian canon is as if you were to expand the phrase "Honor thy father and thy mother" into a whole religion (obviously an oversimplification). There is a common refrain among Chinese Americans I know that their grandparents will guilt them for being single whenever they gather for holidays. Have you ever heard of [Chinese marriage corners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shanghai_Marriage_Market)? Parents meet up to desperately marry off their unmarried children. Row after row of placards read "34. Income 480,000 RMB. 170cm. Has car..."

This is an intensely desperate affair. This is not a market for general marriage, these are parents advertising their unmarried, older children in a desperate attempt to get grandchildren. It is a bit of a sad scene, although spirits were high when I visited. 

In Israel, it's not like this. Israelis are very flirtatious. A stranger in a drugstore tried to set me up with her hotel receptionist. An older religious man on the bus asked my friend if she wanted to marry his son. Multiple women on the train have tried to set me up with young women they know. It's considered auspicious to make a match. When I was asking friends of friends about advice on moving to Israel, one woman assured me that she would set me up with her friend. Israelis approach dating with a fun and flirtatious attitude. They are not doing it to satisfy their parents' demands.

## National pride

I have tried dating both in China and in Israel. It was much, much easier for me in China. This is not very intuitive. I am not Chinese, but I am Jewish. So why should I have an easier time in China?

I believe that there is a component of national pride. Israelis are very proud of their nation. A lot of Jewish Israelis would not marry a non-Jew. I have also gotten the impression that Israelis simply want other Israelis. Israelis love their home and seem to seek people who fit into it well. Confidence and facility in the land of Israel and the Hebrew language are pluses when dating in Israel. Many Israelis envision a future exactly where they are and doing the things that they currently do. 

In China, the opposite is true. Foreigners are deeply fascinating to Chinese people. "Mixed babies" is the kind of thing you might see trending on Chinese social media. There is also a common refrain among many East Asian women that East Asian men are a pain to date. 

This is a concept that is sort of foreign in Israel. I cannot picture in my head a viral TikTok video of an Israeli woman exclaiming that she wants a non-Jewish baby. It's a thought that just doesn't compute. 

## The Asians are Tired

I recently visited Taiwan. I knew that East Asian people work hard in school, but I never realized how intense the work got. It is standard in Taiwan, as it is across much of East Asia, to attend evening cram school every day after school. This means that students are in class from morning until evening. As one Taiwanese friend put it, "I didn't really have my own life until I got to college." 

With respect to this, it is unsurprising that many Taiwanese don't want to have children. One friend I met had a child, but also expressed his reluctance to have another one because he wanted to travel to Japan more. If you have only recently discovered fun and freedom, then why would you be eager to make the sacrifices necessary to raise a family?

Israelis are much more free-spirited than this. Secular Israelis do not fill every hour of their children's days with extra lessons. Israelis are encouraged to travel the world after they finish their army service, something that would be odd for a 21 year-old in Taiwan. 

## Outlook

I think that Israel's high birthrate will begin to decline. Without opining on the birthrates of the ultra orthodox, secular Jews currently foat a bit above replacement level. The population pyramid of Israel shows some signs of slimming. I do not know if Israel can outlast the anti-baby fever that has gripped the world. 

I do think that one of the most important policy issues in Taiwan might be ending the cram school system. It is such a tragedy that so many Taiwanese children languish in classrooms cramming. There is no reason for this. East Asian children in the United States often live much more relaxed lives and still perform well on standardized tests. Especially with the rise of AI, there is no reason to warehouse children in boxes to make them learn. AI tutoring is now too cheap to meter. Taiwan is due for a dramatic cultural liberalization of education. I hope that something like this could have a positive impact on their birthrate. Taiwan is a beautiful and wonderful country, it is a shame to see it hollow out from within.
